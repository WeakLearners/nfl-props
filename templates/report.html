Projected production for each team's featured players. The curve is the full range of plausible outcomes; the shaded band is the middle ~68%, where roughly two of every three games land. The solid upright rule marks the number FanDuel is pricing closest to −__TARGET_ABS__.

Ordered by how close that number sits to the curve's peak. Cards at the top are where the book and the model agree on the likely outcome. Further down, the gap widens — a number well left of the peak is one the model thinks clears more often than the price suggests, and vice versa.

    Reading the matchup figure. 1.15 means this defence gives up 15% more than league average to that position; 0.85 means 15% less. Where it says context only, the projection deliberately ignores it — applying a defensive adjustment to receiving stats made projections measurably worse in testing, because "yards allowed to receivers" mostly records which receivers a defence happened to face.

    How wide the curves are. The spread is widened 10% over the raw model estimate. Measured across 2023–2025, real errors ran about 10% larger than the model's own estimate of them — the raw figure counts a player's game-to-game variation but not the error in the projection itself.

    Model vs book. "Model" is the chance this curve puts on clearing that number. "Book" is what FanDuel's price implies, vig included — so the book's figure is inflated a few points by its own margin, and a small edge to the model is not an edge at all. Tested against real lines, this model was the less accurate of the two; treat a large gap as the model being wrong, not the book.

    What this is not. These are projections, not edges. Tested against real sportsbook lines, this model's point estimates were less accurate than the market's. Useful for seeing a player's plausible range; not evidence anyone is mispriced.

    Two engines, old and new. Every receiving-yards, rushing-yards, and receptions card carries two forecasts for the same player and stat. They come from two different models, built at different times. There is no new-model number for passing yards — the data this page's models train on doesn't have a passing-yards column to fit against, so that card only shows the old engine.

    Old engine (built 2026-09-13, nflprops/project.py). It makes the big number on every card and the range under it. It starts from the player's own recent games, weighting the latest most. It splits his workload (targets, carries) from what he does with each touch (yards per catch or carry). It forecasts each part on its own, multiplies them back together, then adjusts for how tough the opponent's defense is.

    New model (added 2026-09-24, decision #31, also called Phase 3; extended to receptions 2026-09-24, decision #38). It makes the small line under the range, e.g. old engine 60 · new model 52 (−8). For receiving yards and rushing yards it is a ridge regression: a straight-line fit that is penalized for leaning hard on any one input, so it can't memorize noise, trained on 2019–2025 over a wider table of inputs. For receptions it's a different route to the same idea — it predicts how many times a player will be targeted, then multiplies by his own shrunk history of catching the targets he gets. Head-to-head testing found that beat a ridge regression fit directly on receptions, by a hair. Neither engine ever sees the other's number.

    On the curve. The curve belongs to the old engine. Its dashed line, in the team's colour, marks the old engine's number. The purple dashed line marks the new model's. When the two lines sit apart, the engines disagree. The shortlist shows the same pair, in the same colours.

    Reading the gap. The number in brackets is new minus old. A minus sign means the new model expects less. The two are never averaged into one figure. The line is shaded when the gap is more than half the card's spread. The spread is the distance from the big number to either end of its range. Say a card reads 60, range 40–80: the spread is 20, so a gap of 10 or more gets shaded. A gap that size is bigger than the old engine's own uncertainty, so the card deserves a second look.

    Which one to trust. Neither, clearly. Scored on five seasons of real games (2021–2025, data/vol_head_to_head.csv and data/vol_receptions_compare.csv), all three are close to a tie. On receiving yards the old engine misses by 22.82 yards on average, the new model by 22.84 (12,040 games). On rushing yards it is 21.30 against 21.44 (7,332 games). On receptions it is 1.65 against 1.65 (12,040 games, sub-hundredth difference). Season by season, the new model won receiving in 3 of 5 years, rushing in 2 of 5, and receptions in 3 of 5. The new model is better at the raw counts underneath: how many targets and carries a player gets. This page doesn't show those counts. The old engine keeps the headline on every stat because it hasn't been beaten, not because it clearly won.

    Touchdowns work the same way. The td % badge is the old engine's anytime-touchdown number. The smaller new td % under it is the new model's (decision #30). In each game, both models name the player most likely to score. Over 2021–2025 the old engine's choice scored 45% of the time, the new model's 41%. So the old number stays primary.
