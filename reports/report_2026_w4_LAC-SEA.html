    Reading the matchup line. It shows where the opponent ranks against that position, from 1st (toughest) to 32nd (softest), by yards allowed per game, adjusted for the offenses each defense faced and weighted toward recent games. It is one rank for the whole position, the same rank the Big day and Caution badges use. The range comes from the model, which uses its own defense number, and the matchup line is the adjusted defense rank.

    Older reports show a ratio instead: 1.15 means that defense gives up 15% more than league average to that position. Where it says context only, the projection ignores it.

    How wide the curves are. The spread is widened 10% over the raw model estimate. Measured across 2023–2025, real errors ran about 10% larger than the model's own estimate of them — the raw figure counts a player's game-to-game variation but not the error in the projection itself.

    Model vs book. "Model" is the chance this curve puts on clearing that number. "Book" is what FanDuel's price implies, vig included — so the book's figure is inflated a few points by its own margin, and a small edge to the model is not an edge at all. Tested against real lines, this model was the less accurate of the two; treat a large gap as the model being wrong, not the book.

    What this is not. These are projections, not edges. Tested against real sportsbook lines, this model's point estimates were less accurate than the market's. Useful for seeing a player's plausible range; not evidence anyone is mispriced.

    Two engines, old and new. Every passing-yards, receiving-yards, rushing-yards, and receptions card carries two forecasts for the same player and stat. They come from two different models, built at different times.

    Old engine (built 2026-09-13, nflprops/project.py). It makes the big number on every card and the range under it. It starts from the player's own recent games, weighting the latest most. It splits his workload (targets, carries, pass attempts) from what he does with each touch (yards per catch, carry, or attempt). It forecasts each part on its own, multiplies them back together, then adjusts for how tough the opponent's defense is.

    New model (added 2026-09-24, decision #31, also called Phase 3; extended to receptions 2026-09-24, decision #38, and to passing yards 2026-09-24, decision #39). It makes the small line under the range, e.g. old engine 60 · new model 52 (−8). For passing yards, receiving yards, and rushing yards it is a ridge regression: a straight-line fit that is penalized for leaning hard on any one input, so it can't memorize noise, trained on 2019–2025 over a wider table of inputs (passing yards adds each QB's own attempts, completions, yards per attempt, completion rate, and downfield throw depth, each averaged over his last 4 games, last 8, and career). For receptions it's a different route to the same idea — it predicts how many times a player will be targeted, then multiplies by his own shrunk history of catching the targets he gets. Head-to-head testing found that beat a ridge regression fit directly on receptions, by a hair. Neither engine ever sees the other's number.

    On the curve. The curve belongs to the old engine. Its dashed line, in the team's colour, marks the old engine's number. The purple dashed line marks the new model's. When the two lines sit apart, the engines disagree. The shortlist shows the same pair, in the same colours.

    Reading the gap. The number in brackets is new minus old. A minus sign means the new model expects less. The two are never averaged into one figure. The line is shaded when the gap is more than half the card's spread. The spread is the distance from the big number to either end of its range. Say a card reads 60, range 40–80: the spread is 20, so a gap of 10 or more gets shaded. A gap that size is bigger than the old engine's own uncertainty, so the card deserves a second look.

    Which one to trust. Mixed. Scored on five seasons of real games (2021–2025, data/vol_head_to_head.csv, data/vol_receptions_compare.csv, and data/passing_head_to_head.csv), receiving yards, rushing yards, and receptions are all close to a tie. On receiving yards the old engine misses by 22.82 yards on average, the new model by 22.84 (12,040 games). On rushing yards it is 21.30 against 21.44 (7,332 games). On receptions it is 1.65 against 1.65 (12,040 games, sub-hundredth difference). Season by season, the new model won receiving in 3 of 5 years, rushing in 2 of 5, and receptions in 3 of 5. Passing yards is not a tie. The old engine misses by 67.09 yards on average; the new model misses by 62.56 (3,107 games, 2021–2025) — a 6.8% smaller miss, and the new model came out ahead in every one of the five seasons tested. The old engine still makes the big number on the card, same as every other stat here, because that's this page's fixed convention — not because it won this one. The new model is also better at the raw counts underneath the other three stats: how many targets and carries a player gets. This page doesn't show those counts.

    Touchdowns work the same way. The td % badge is the old engine's anytime-touchdown number. The smaller new td % under it is the new model's (decision #30). In each game, both models name the player most likely to score. Over 2021–2025 the old engine's choice scored 45% of the time, the new model's 41%. So the old number stays primary.
